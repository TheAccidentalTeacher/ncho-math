The plan

Five steps, in order

Each one unblocks the next. Step one is the only thing standing between here and a math book a week — and it’s also the engine an app would run on, so it gets built once and pays twice.

  1. Teach it to invent its own problems

    Right now every problem is hand-written and then verified. Fine for a sample. Useless for 53 standards, let alone a book a week.

    What’s needed is a template: “a proportion with a whole-number answer” that produces a hundred versions, all with numbers that come out clean, none of them accidentally broken — no dividing by zero, no negative lengths, no ugly decimals where whole numbers were intended.

    The trick the established systems use is backwards: pick the answer first, then build a problem around it. Never generate randomly and hope something good falls out.

    The real blocker · roughly a day · unblocks everything below

  2. Plug math into the actual book machine

    Everything built so far is standalone — it makes a beautiful sample that isn’t connected to the engine that makes the books already selling.

    Connecting them means adding math question types alongside the eight word types, and two fields to the lesson format: one for the math, one for the figure.

    Half a day, once step 1 exists

  3. Wire the standards in

    The 664-code table exists and the checker works. What’s missing is running it against each lesson as it’s written, under the same three-tier honesty rule the science books already use: aligned, supporting, or no code at all with a written reason.

    On TPT: tag Common Core and let their crosswalk translate to state standards for buyers automatically. They explicitly can’t crosswalk Texas or Virginia, so those get tagged by hand or not at all.

    A couple of hours

  4. Build the correction channel

    Since nobody ships clean, the thing that makes shipping safe is a way to fix a mistake in public.

    Copy OpenStax’s model — the only process in all this research with real outcome data behind it. Typos route straight through with no review. Anything needing judgment goes to a human. The whole log is public. That turns an inevitable defect into visible diligence instead of a one-star review.

    A day · do it before volume, not after

  5. Make it fast

    Every figure currently starts a fresh program from cold — about half a second of pure startup doing nothing. Keeping one running instead measures roughly nine times faster.

    For a 200-figure book that’s twelve seconds instead of a hundred. Irrelevant at one book. Very relevant at five a week.

    Two hours · do it when volume makes it hurt
Scott’s call, 4 September

Half of what we closed reopens when we build apps

Everything in the research was decided for paper — a book Amazon prints, a PDF a teacher downloads. Several tools got ruled out on that basis and nothing else.

That’s a snapshot, not a law. “Wrong for paper” and “wrong for us” are different verdicts, and only one is permanent.

FREE
MIT LICENCE

JSXGraph — interactive geometry

Skipped because print doesn’t need it. But it isn’t a figure library — it’s a manipulative. Drag a vertex, watch the angle update. Real objects for angles, arcs, bisectors, tangents, circumcircles and congruence marks, with exact measurements available at any moment.

University-built, shipping releases in July and August, and licensed so commercial use costs nothing.

FREE
MIT LICENCE

Khan Academy’s exercise engine

Khan open-sources the actual thing that renders and grades every problem on their site. Version 87 shipped this week — alive, not abandoned. For an exercise app, that’s about a year of work you don’t do.

ALREADY
BUILT

Live answer checking

The checker already decides whether a kid’s 1.5 means the same as the key’s 3/2. On paper that’s a build-time check on a fixed key. In an app it’s a student typing and being told. Same code, different product.

ALREADY
PLANNED

Infinite practice

Step one makes twenty problems once, for a book. In an app the same machine makes a fresh one every time a child gets one wrong. That is, precisely and entirely, IXL’s business model.

FREE
+ A MOAT

Math a screen reader can read

Worthless in a PDF. In an app it’s real accessibility — and it’s the gap the market leader publicly admits to: Kuta’s own accessibility page says screen readers cannot describe their diagrams.

FLIPS
PAID TIER

Desmos — and this one is almost funny

Closed because its terms forbid reproducing graphs “other than through the Applications.” A book isn’t an Application. An app is. That is the exact case the licence was written for.

Still needs a paid commercial tier, price unpublished — a negotiation, not a checkout button. But it moved from prohibited to available.

STILL
CLOSED

GeoGebra

Their rule isn’t about format: “whether a use is non-commercial depends on the use, not the user.” A commercial app is commercial use. Same negotiated publisher contract, with an audit clause.

STILL
CLOSED

Formal proof software

Not a paper-versus-screen thing — it isn’t ready. The step that translates a problem into the prover’s language runs 10–66% accurate, and human experts get it wrong 38% of the time. It would certify wrong answers with total confidence.

The rule this produced

When something gets ruled out, say which frame it failed — paper, licence, maturity, or fit. A verdict that was really about paper must never quietly harden into a permanent no.

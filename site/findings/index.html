Five research passes, September 2026

What we learned

Everything below was checked against a real source, not assumed. The uncomfortable parts are here too — including the places we were wrong.

The benchmark nobody publishes

Nobody ships a clean math book

OpenStax runs a public error tracker. Eleven professionally produced math titles — peer reviewed, edited, paid accuracy checkers, the whole apparatus.

OPENSTAX MATHEMATICS — every error report ever filed, as of 2026-09-04 reports submitted . . . . . . . . 4,996 confirmed real errors . . . . . . 3,343 confirmed WRONG ANSWERS . . . . . . 1,187 Calculus Volume 3 alone: 1,053 confirmed errors, 341 wrong answers Developmental math books: wrong answers are ~50% of every defect Median time to fix: 140 days

That’s what “professionally checked” actually delivers. And it’s freeing, not frightening: the question was never whether we can ship zero errors — nobody can. The question is whether there’s a way to fix one when it’s found. That’s a much smaller thing to build.

A true sentence for a product listing: our answer key is computed by a computer algebra system and cross-checked by a second independent method. That is already better than what the textbook industry does.

A thing everyone believes

“Two independent solvers” is folklore

Ask anyone how textbook answer keys get verified and you’ll hear that two different people solve everything independently. There is no source for that anywhere. The phrases aren’t even industry vocabulary.

The one book preface that actually spells out who did what credits the same person for preparing the answer key and serving as accuracy checker. One person, working forward, twice. The pattern repeats across at least five publishers.

The one scope-of-work document we found priced accuracy-checking an entire book at $250–750. That’s the industry’s real spend.

And the reason the role exists at all isn’t craft pride — it’s that Texas law makes publishers swear an affidavit that their materials are free of factual error, with penalties attached.

Legal, not technical

Two graphing tools we cannot touch

Desmos

The obvious tool. Its terms forbid reproducing its graphs “other than through the Applications” — and a printed book isn’t an Application. Free tier is non-commercial only, and their branding can’t be removed either.

Reopens for apps, where an app is an Application — with a paid commercial tier attached.

GeoGebra

Technically the best of them. Its licence names our exact use twice, in language updated November 2025: “educational materials or resources which will be sold in exchange for a fee,” and “within a non-academic ebook.”

Stays closed even for apps — their bar is commercial use, not format.

Texas standards: the code, never the text

Texas forbids charging for “any document containing” their standards text unless you’re a Texas district or resident. Alaska fits no exemption.

But a code like 7.3(A) is a legal citation — a fact, and facts aren’t ownable. So: codes yes, text never. Costs nothing to obey.

Common Core: free, with an exact line

No fee, no permission. But any product printing the standard’s text carries this, word for word:

© Copyright 2010. National Governors Association Center for Best Practices and Council of Chief State School Officers. All rights reserved.

The “For example…” bits inside standards were licensed from third parties and aren’t covered.

Time-sensitive

The free textbooks are disappearing

OpenStax math books you can legally build a sold product from are the first editions. They’re being replaced by second editions licensed non-commercial — useless to us. Two already return “page not found.” Only 10 of their 24 math titles were ever open enough.

The one to actually build from: Illustrative Mathematics 6–12 through Kendall Hunt. Licensed CC BY 4.0, covers middle and high school, and they explicitly permit selling what you derive from it.

Avoid: the WeBWorK problem library (non-commercial, and it contains folders named after publishers whose rights they couldn’t have licensed), CK-12 (no longer open), EngageNY (non-commercial), and Kuta (their licence claims the output).

Watch ShareAlike, not just NonCommercial. A CC BY-SA licence reads like a green light and would force a $9.99 book to be redistributable free.

Not about math at all

Your landlord owns your competition

IXL Learning owns Teachers Pay Teachers. They also own Carson Dellosa and Evan-Moor — two of the largest print-workbook publishers in the country — plus Rosetta Stone, Education.com, ABCya and Wyzant.

So the storefront we sell on is owned by the company that owns the shelf we compete with.

And here’s the good part. Across all 111 of their open jobs they’re hiring freelance worksheet designers who need “expert-level InDesign and Illustrator.” There is not one mention of any automated figure generation, anywhere. Their math figures are drawn by hand, by people, one at a time. That’s a labour cost, not a technology moat.

The risk runs backwards

Amazon enforces. TPT doesn’t.

Amazon KDP

Three severity tiers, customer error reporting, a dashboard that emails you monthly until you fix it, a quality warning banner on your public product page, and — their words — “books with wrong content are always removed from sale.”

Teachers Pay Teachers

Terms disclaim all warranty on accuracy. Sellers warrant their age, billing, tax and IP — nothing about correctness. The refund policy has exactly five grounds and “the answer key is wrong” isn’t one of them.

Which makes TPT sound safer. It isn’t. On Amazon the platform makes you fix it. On TPT the buyer is a teacher who finds your mistake in front of thirty children and writes it in a public rating. Zero platform risk; the highest reputation risk anywhere.

The one to design around

Don’t let the AI grade its own homework

If a model writes the lesson text, something reads the answer back out of that text, and the checker verifies it against assumptions also pulled from that same text — every check goes green while proving only that the model agreed with itself.

The green report makes the error look impossible. That’s worse than no checking.

The fix is architecture, not a better checker

The numbers are decided first, mechanically. The model never writes a number. Its prose is treated as untrusted forever. Verification computes by a second independent route — build the problem backwards from the answer, or substitute the answer back in and check it lands.

Checked, and it didn’t bite

The font scare

Research flagged that Chrome quietly converts certain fonts into a format that’s really just a pile of drawings with no letters behind it. Printers reject those, and every normal tool reports them as fine.

So we checked every product currently for sale: Wren & Sitka’s interior and cover, all four Constitution Day files, all ten study guides. Every one clean. There’s a permanent check now so it stays that way.

Where we were wrong

Three corrections

  • “The typesetter doesn’t emit vector drawings.” It does — every square root, short ones included. Still fine for our purposes, but the claim was wrong and got measured and corrected.
  • “The standards table has to be built by hand.” It didn’t. There’s a real machine-readable release, and the 664-code table now sits in the repo because of it.
  • “These tools are ruled out.” Several were ruled out for paper, and that got written down as if it were permanent. Half of them reopen the day we build an app. The split is here.
